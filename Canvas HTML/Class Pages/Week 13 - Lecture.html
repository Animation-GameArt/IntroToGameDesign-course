Exercise: Ex 10 — Playtest. Three classmates play; the developer watches silently, takes notes, and turns the notes into three prioritized changes.

Also due: nothing this week. Students bring a build that runs on a lab computer.

Lecture topics, expanded

Why you can't test your own game. The developer has perfect knowledge of the game and so is the worst possible tester of whether it explains itself. Show a short clip, or act it out: a player stuck on something the developer thought was obvious. Every student will see this happen to their own game within the hour, and it's better to expect it.

Running a session. Give the player one sentence: "Play this, and say what you're thinking." Then stay silent. No hints, no apologies, no "oh, that's a bug." Take notes in three columns: where they hesitated, what they tried that didn't work, and where they smiled or leaned in. Ten minutes per player is enough. Thank them, then ask the three prepared questions.

Doing and saying. Players are polite, and they're poor at explaining their own behavior. "It was fun" from a player who quit after two minutes means less than the quitting does. Weigh what was observed above what was said. When three players in a row get stuck at the same spot, that spot is the problem, whatever they say about it.

Reading difficulty. Mark where each player failed, and how many tries each section took. A section nobody fails may be too easy or may just be a rest. A section everyone fails five times is a wall. Compare this with the difficulty the student intended in their ten-pager. The fix is usually a number or a checkpoint, not a new feature.

Connecting it forward

The three changes chosen today are the first things checked in the Beta build. The bugs found go on the list that next week's bug bash adds to.
