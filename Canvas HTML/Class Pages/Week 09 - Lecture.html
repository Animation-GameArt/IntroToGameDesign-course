Also due: nothing new this week. First Playable is due the night before Week 10.

Lecture topics, expanded

What to save, and when. Saving is a design question before it's a technical one. Ask what the player would be upset to lose: progress through levels, a high score, unlocked items, their settings. Then decide when it's saved. Saving at checkpoints and at the end of a level is predictable and safe. Many small games only need a high score and settings, and that's a fine answer.

The settings players expect. Volume for music and for sound effects, and a way to quit. Players notice when these are missing more than they notice most features. Show where the settings live in the course project's menus, and that they're saved between sessions.

Making a build and publishing it. A build is the game packaged to run without Unity. Walk through Build Profiles: the Scene List decides which scenes are included and which comes first, and the platform is Windows or Web. Then upload to itch.io as a private page. The first build nearly always shows a surprise: a scene missing from the list, a menu that doesn't fit the screen, a file path that only worked in the Editor. Finding that in Week 9 is the reason this isn't left until Week 15.

How to watch someone play. Students have played their own game hundreds of times and can no longer see it fresh. The rule for a playtest is simple and hard: give the player the game, and say nothing. Watch where they hesitate, what they try that doesn't work, and where they give up. What a player does is better evidence than what they say afterwards. A two-minute test in lab today is practice for the full playtest in Week 13.

Connecting it forward
