# Scope

**Week 2 · [[Lecture] Week 2](https://github.com/Animation-GameArt/IntroToGameDesign-course/blob/main/Class%20Pages/Week%2002%20-%20Lecture.md)**

> [!abstract] In one sentence
> Scope is how much game you plan to make, and a smaller game that is finished beats a bigger game that isn't.

## What this topic is for

Over-scoping is the most common reason student games don't get finished. This lecture gives students concrete measures for "small enough" before they pitch, and the habit of separating what the game needs from what would be nice.

## Key ideas

- **The real budget.** Production runs from Week 8 to Week 15: eight weeks, one person, alongside other classes and work. Ask students to estimate their hours per week honestly, then multiply.
- **Count everything.** Every level, enemy, sprite, sound, menu and line of dialogue has to be made, placed and tested. Listing them makes size visible.
- **Must have and nice to have.** Must-have features are the ones the game fails without. Everything else is nice to have, and is cut first. Page 10 of the ten-pager asks for both lists.
- **One thing done well.** One core mechanic, one level finished before a second is started, one enemy that works before a second is added.
- **The genre map is a scope tool.** The kits already contain the main mechanics, so the scope that matters is content and the game's own twist.
- **Scope changes.** Cutting a feature during production is a professional skill, not a failure. The earlier the cut, the cheaper it is.

## In class

1. Take a well-known game and estimate its content: levels, enemies, art. Then show the size of its team and its schedule.
2. Do the same for a student-sized version of the same game.
3. Have each student draft a must-have list of five items for one of their ideas.

## Common problems

| Students often… | Help them by… |
|---|---|
| Plan many levels | Asking for one level, finished and tested, then a count of how long it took |
| Add a feature for every genre they like | Pointing to the one genre that describes most of the play |
| Treat cutting as failing | Showing cuts from published games' development stories |
